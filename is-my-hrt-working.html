Is my HRT working? How to tell, and how long it takes

A few weeks in, you catch yourself wondering: is this actually doing anything, or am I just hoping it is? Maybe the nights are a little better, maybe they're not, and it's hard to tell real change from wishful thinking. That uncertainty is one of the most common feelings in early HRT — so let's make it easier to answer honestly.

A quick note: this is general information, not medical advice. If your HRT doesn't feel right, don't just put up with it or quietly stop — talk to your prescriber, because dose and type can often be adjusted.

How long before HRT starts working?

Some things shift quite quickly and others take their time, which is part of why it feels confusing. As a rough guide: hot flushes and night sweats often ease within the first two to six weeks. Sleep, mood, energy and brain fog tend to follow more gradually, and the fuller picture — including getting the dose right — can take up to about three months. So if you're two weeks in and underwhelmed, you're not failing at this, and neither is the HRT. It's often just early.

Signs your HRT is working

Improvement usually arrives quietly, as an absence of things rather than a fanfare. You might notice:

  • Fewer or milder hot flushes and night sweats
  • Sleeping through more often, or dropping back off more easily
  • A steadier mood — less of the sudden tears or short fuse
  • A bit more energy, and a mind that feels less foggy
  • Less joint stiffness, and often improvements in dryness and libido over a longer stretch

Rarely do all of these land at once. More often it's one, then another, creeping in over weeks.

Why it's genuinely hard to tell

Here's the catch: progress is gradual, but memory is a terrible instrument for measuring gradual change. One bad night can make the whole fortnight feel like a write-off, even if most nights quietly improved. We judge how we're doing by how we feel today, which is exactly why so many people feel stuck — the change is real, but it's invisible from inside the moment.

When your HRT might not be right (yet)

If you're past about three months and still struggling, that's worth acting on — it doesn't mean HRT has failed, often it means the details need tweaking. The dose might be too low, the type or delivery method (patch, gel, tablet) might suit you better in another form, or the progesterone part might need adjusting. These are common, fixable conversations. Please don't suffer in silence or stop abruptly — go back and say "this isn't quite there yet."

The honest way to know

The trick is to stop relying on today's mood and start watching the trend. Jot a quick line each day — how you slept, how you felt, any flushes — next to what you took. After a few weeks you can look back and see it in black and white: fewer bad nights, steadier days, the slow climb you couldn't feel while you were living it. It turns "I think maybe?" into something real — and it gives your prescriber something to work with instead of a shrug.

Common questions

How long does HRT take to work?

Hot flushes and night sweats often ease within about two to six weeks, while sleep, mood, energy and brain fog usually improve more gradually. Getting the dose right and seeing the fuller benefit can take up to around three months, so feeling underwhelmed early on is common and does not mean it is failing.

What are the signs that HRT is working?

Improvement tends to arrive quietly, as fewer or milder hot flushes and night sweats, sleeping through more often, a steadier mood, more energy and less brain fog, and over a longer stretch, improvements in dryness and libido. It is normal for one thing to improve before another rather than everything at once.

Why does it feel like my HRT is not working?

Progress is gradual but memory judges you by how you feel today, so one bad night can make a quietly improving fortnight feel like a failure. Keeping a short daily note lets you look back and see the real trend, which is often better than it feels in the moment.

What should I do if my HRT is not working after three months?

Go back to your prescriber rather than stopping abruptly. It rarely means HRT has failed, more often the dose, the type or delivery method, or the progesterone part needs adjusting. Bringing a record of your symptoms makes it much easier to fine-tune.
