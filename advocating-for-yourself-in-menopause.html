When no one believes you: advocating for yourself in menopause

“It’s probably just stress.” “Your bloods are normal.” “You’re a bit young for that.” If you’ve heard these while quietly falling apart, this is for you. Being your own advocate isn’t being difficult — it’s refusing to be erased. Here’s how to do it well.

A quick note: this is general, educational information, not medical advice. Decisions about HRT, medication and your health should be made with your own prescriber — always follow their guidance and ask your pharmacist if you’re unsure.

Know the facts you’re standing on

Confidence comes from knowing the ground is solid. In the UK, for most women over 45, perimenopause and menopause are diagnosed from symptoms and age — not a single blood test, which can be misleading because hormones swing day to day. So “your bloods are normal” does not rule menopause out. Knowing that one fact changes how you walk into the room.

Bring evidence, not just feelings

On a hard day, your own account can come out jumbled, and that makes you easier to dismiss. A written record does the arguing for you: these symptoms, this frequency, this long, this is the impact on my work and family. Patterns on paper are harder to wave away than words in a difficult ten-minute appointment.

Ask direct questions

Direct questions invite real answers. “Based on my symptoms, could this be perimenopause?” “What are my options, including HRT?” “If not now, what would need to change for us to revisit this?” Writing the answers down keeps everyone accountable and gives you something to build on.

Second opinions are your right

If you feel genuinely unheard, you can ask to see a different clinician at the practice, ideally one with a menopause interest, or ask about a referral. Seeking another opinion isn’t rude — it’s what you’d tell your best friend to do.

Hold your line, kindly

You can be calm and still be firm. “I understand, and I’d still like to explore this further” is a complete sentence. You are the expert on your own body. The more clearly you can show what you’re living, the harder it is for anyone to tell you it isn’t happening.

Start building that evidence today — your symptoms, your treatments and supplements, your daily reality — so the next time someone reaches for “it’s probably nothing,” you have the whole picture in your hand.

Common questions

Can I have menopause if my blood test is normal?

Yes. For most women over 45, menopause and perimenopause are diagnosed from symptoms and age, not a single blood test — hormone levels swing, so a ‘normal’ result doesn’t rule it out.

What should I bring to a menopause appointment?

A written list of your symptoms, how long you’ve had them, how often they happen and how they affect your life, plus any treatments and supplements you take. Evidence is much harder to dismiss than a verbal summary.

Can I ask for a second opinion about menopause?

Yes. If you feel unheard, it’s reasonable to ask to see another clinician with a menopause interest or to ask about a referral. Seeking another opinion is your right.
