Is it perimenopause, or something else?

Knackered all the time, flat in your mood, a brain that keeps dropping words. In your forties the easy answer is perimenopause, and often that is exactly what it is. But those same symptoms can come from a handful of other things that are very treatable once someone looks, and that is worth knowing before you file it all under hormones.

A quick note: this is general, educational writing, not medical advice. Anything to do with HRT, medication or a diagnosis belongs with your own GP or specialist, so please take your questions to them.

The great symptom overlap

Fatigue, low mood, poor concentration, weight changes, disrupted sleep: these turn up in perimenopause, but they also turn up in several other conditions that become more common in midlife. That overlap is why some women get told it is “just hormones” when something else is also going on, and occasionally why something hormonal gets missed because everyone is looking elsewhere.

Things worth ruling out

Without turning you into your own doctor, a few causes come up often enough to be worth a mention to your GP:

  • thyroid problems, which can mimic a lot of menopause symptoms
  • low iron or anaemia, especially if your periods have been heavy
  • low vitamin D or B12
  • depression or anxiety as conditions in their own right
  • diabetes, sleep disorders, and other things a simple check can flag

Most of these are picked up with a conversation and some straightforward tests, and most are very treatable once named.

It can be more than one thing

This is the bit that trips people up. It is not always perimenopause or something else. You can be perimenopausal and have an underactive thyroid and be low on iron, all at once, each adding to the heap. Sorting out one can make a real dent even if the others remain, so it is worth untangling rather than assuming a single cause explains everything.

Why a record helps the diagnosis

When your symptoms are vague and all-over-the-place, a good history is one of the most useful things you can give a clinician. If you can show when symptoms started, whether they track with your cycle, how your sleep and energy have shifted and what you have already tried, you make it far easier for them to spot what fits perimenopause and what does not. You also make it less likely to be sent away with a shrug.

What to do next

Book the appointment, and do not downplay it when you get there; say clearly how much this is affecting your life. Ask directly whether it could be perimenopause and whether anything else should be checked. Bring your notes. Whatever the answer turns out to be, you will have lost nothing by looking, and you might get a chunk of your life back faster.

If you have not been keeping track, start today: symptoms, sleep, energy, cycle, and anything you are already taking. A few weeks of that is worth more at your appointment than the best memory on a tired day.

Common questions

Could my symptoms be something other than perimenopause?

Possibly. Tiredness, low mood and brain fog overlap with thyroid problems, low iron, low vitamin D or B12, depression and other conditions that are common in midlife. It is worth asking your GP to consider both perimenopause and other causes.

Can I have perimenopause and another condition at the same time?

Yes — it is common to have more than one thing going on at once, such as perimenopause alongside an underactive thyroid or low iron. Treating one can help even if the others remain, so it is worth untangling rather than assuming a single cause.

What should I take to my GP appointment?

A record of when your symptoms started, whether they track with your cycle, how your sleep and energy have changed, and anything you have already tried. A clear history makes it much easier to work out what is perimenopause and what is not.
