Silver or transparent? The patch I could never remember

A menopause story 4 min read

It's Thursday. Or — is it a patch day? I'm standing in the bathroom in the ninety seconds I've carved out before the school run, holding the little box, and I genuinely cannot tell you whether the thing on my hip went on Monday and is due to come off, or went on Thursday last week and I've somehow been wearing it for a fortnight.

I peel back the waistband and squint. There's a patch there. It's a bit grey at the edges, curling where my jeans rub. Is that four days of wear, or seven? I press it back down like that will tell me something. It does not.

I take two patches a week. You'd think two things would be easy to hold in your head. Reader, they are not.

The plan that never survives real life

Every single time, I make the same promise: I'll just remember. Mondays and Thursdays. Simple. And every single time, life happens between the intention and the patch. A work call runs over. My son can't find his other shoe. My mum rings about her appointment and I'm suddenly the family switchboard, and by the time I sit down it's 9pm and the last thing my brain offers up is which day I last changed a small square of plastic on my body.

So I decide to write it down. Of course I do. I find a pen, I write "patch — Mon/Thu" on the back of an envelope, and I put it… somewhere sensible. Which is to say, I never see that envelope again. The next week I write it on a sticky note stuck to the mirror, where it lives until it falls behind the radiator. I have, at various points, tried to remember by association — "I change it on bin day" — right up until the bank holiday moved bin day and took my whole system down with it.

Twice a week is a trap for a busy brain

Here's the cruel bit. A daily thing, I could manage — you do it, it's done, tomorrow you do it again. But twice a week? Every-few-days lives in exactly the blind spot my memory can't cover. Was it three days ago or four? Did I actually change it, or did I just think about changing it so vividly that it feels like I did? By the middle of the second week I've genuinely lost the thread.

And it's not helped by the fact that the very tool I'm relying on — my own memory — is the thing menopause has quietly turned down. The brain fog is real. I've walked into rooms and forgotten why. I've called the dog by my daughter's name. Asking that same foggy brain to keep a precise two-day rota, indefinitely, with no help, was never going to work. It wasn't a character flaw. It was a bad system.

Silver, or the clear one?

Then there was the fortnight I switched brands. My usual came in a silver box; the pharmacy gave me a different one — the transparent kind you can barely see on your skin. Sensible, except now, mid-week, I couldn't even use "can I see it?" as a clue, because half the time I couldn't. Silver or transparent? Did I open the new box yet or am I still on the old? I stood there doing detective work on my own hip, and honestly, I laughed, because the alternative was to cry a little.

What I didn't love was the low hum of worry underneath the comedy. Am I giving myself too much? Too little? If I double up by accident, does that matter? If I've been wearing a spent patch for three extra days, is that why I feel off? I even — and I'm not proud of this — fished a wrapper out of the bin once to check the date I'd scribbled on it. That was the morning I thought: this cannot be the system. Not for something that's supposed to be helping me feel like myself again.

The problem was never me. The problem was asking a tired brain to be a filing cabinet.

What actually fixed it

The fix, when it came, was almost annoyingly simple: I stopped trusting my memory and gave the job to something that doesn't get tired. Now, when I change a patch, I tap it once — done, logged, with the day and the time. When I open the bathroom cupboard the next morning, one glance tells me whether anything's due today. No maths. No detective work on my own waistband. No envelope.

The relief was bigger than I expected. It wasn't just the practical bit — although never again standing there guessing is worth a lot. It was putting down the low-level anxiety I'd been carrying around for months without quite naming it. The little running tab of did I, didn't I just… switched off. I hadn't realised how much quiet space it was taking up until it was gone.

If you're reading this nodding — if you've got a curling patch and a lost sticky note and a brain that won't hold the one thing you need it to — I promise it isn't you. You're doing a lot. You just need the remembering taken off your plate.
