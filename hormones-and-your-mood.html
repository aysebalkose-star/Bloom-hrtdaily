Your hormones and your mood: the link nobody explains

One minute you’re fine, the next you’re crying at an advert or snapping at someone you love — then drowning in guilt. If your moods feel like they belong to someone else lately, there’s a reason, and it isn’t that you’ve become “difficult.” It’s your hormones, and it’s real.

A quick note: this is general, educational information, not medical advice. Decisions about HRT, medication and your health should be made with your own prescriber — always follow their guidance and ask your pharmacist if you’re unsure.

The link nobody explains

Women are often told their mood changes are stress, personality or “just life” — rarely that hormones are involved. Yet the connection is real and recognised. Understanding it can lift an enormous weight of self-blame: you’re not a worse person lately, you’re a person navigating a hormonal storm.

Why oestrogen moves your mood

Oestrogen interacts with brain chemicals involved in mood and wellbeing, including serotonin. So when oestrogen falls and fluctuates, mood can dip and swing right along with it. This is biology doing what biology does — not a measure of your strength or character.

Why it swings so much

In perimenopause especially, hormones don’t glide gently down — they lurch and spike unpredictably. That turbulence is why mood can feel so volatile and hard to predict: good days and crashing days in the same week, seemingly out of nowhere. There often is a pattern underneath, even when it feels like chaos.

Finding your pattern

This is where tracking is genuinely revealing. Note your mood each day alongside your cycle, sleep and treatments, and over a few weeks a shape often appears — dips clustering at certain cycle points, worse moods after bad sleep, better stretches with certain changes. Seeing the pattern makes the lows less frightening and easier to plan around.

Getting support

Understanding the link is empowering, but you don’t have to white-knuckle it. If low mood is persistent or severe — or you ever have thoughts of harming yourself — please speak to your GP or urgent help now. Perimenopausal mood changes are treatable, and a clear record of your mood pattern helps your clinician help you faster.

Start today: track your mood with your sleep, cycle, treatments and daily life, find your pattern, and turn “what is wrong with me?” into “here’s what’s happening, and here’s what helps.”

Common questions

Can hormones cause mood swings in menopause?

Yes — oestrogen interacts with mood-related brain chemistry like serotonin, so as it falls and fluctuates, mood can dip and swing. Menopausal mood changes are biological and recognised, not a weakness.

Why are my moods worse in perimenopause?

In perimenopause, hormones fluctuate sharply and unpredictably rather than declining smoothly, which can make mood feel especially volatile. Tracking often reveals a pattern underneath the chaos.

When should I seek help for menopausal mood changes?

If low mood is persistent or severe, or you have any thoughts of harming yourself, contact your GP or urgent help straight away. Perimenopausal mood changes are treatable and you deserve support.
