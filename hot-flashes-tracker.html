Tracking your hot flashes: finding the pattern

It arrives without warning — a rush of heat up the neck, a prickle of sweat, that urge to fling off a layer in the middle of a meeting or a good night's sleep. Hot flashes (or hot flushes, if you're in the UK) can feel random and maddening. But they're rarely as random as they seem, and when you start writing them down, a surprising amount of order appears.

A quick note: this is general information, not medical advice. If flushes are wearing you down, they're very treatable — it's well worth a conversation with your GP or prescriber.

Why hot flashes happen

As estrogen falls, the part of your brain that acts as your internal thermostat gets twitchy. It starts misreading small rises in body temperature as "too hot" and slams on the cooling: blood vessels near the skin open up, you flush, you sweat, and sometimes your heart races. It's not in your head and it's not weakness — it's a thermostat that's lost some of its calibration. At night, the same thing becomes the drenching sweats that wake you.

Why tracking them actually helps

When flushes feel random, they feel uncontrollable — and that's half of what makes them so demoralising. Writing them down flips that. Patterns you'd never spot in the moment start to show: the glass of red wine that reliably sets one off, the cluster that follows a bad night's sleep, the way they ease a few weeks after starting or adjusting HRT. Even just seeing them written down takes some of their power away, because now they're a thing you're observing rather than a thing happening to you.

What to note down

Keep it light — you're not writing a dissertation. A useful entry might include:

  • When it happened (time of day, or "woke me at 3am")
  • How strong it felt — a quick mild/moderate/severe is plenty
  • What came just before — a hot drink, stress, a warm room, alcohol
  • How you slept, if it was a night sweat

A line a day builds a picture faster than you'd think.

Common triggers to watch for

Everyone's different, but these come up again and again — see which are yours:

  • Alcohol, especially red wine
  • Caffeine and very hot drinks
  • Spicy food
  • Stress and strong emotion
  • Warm rooms, hot baths, and heavy bedding
  • A poor night's sleep the night before

You don't have to give up everything on the list — just knowing your triggers lets you choose your moments.

What tends to help

Layered, breathable clothing and a cooler bedroom make the day-to-day more bearable. Cutting back on your personal triggers helps. Paced, slow breathing at the first sign of a flush can ease it. And for many people, HRT reduces flushes significantly — tracking is how you'll see whether it's working for you, and how quickly. If flushes are affecting your life, that's reason enough to ask for help, not something to simply endure.

Common questions

How do I track my hot flashes?

Keep it light, a line a day is plenty. Note when each flush happened, how strong it felt, what came just before it such as a hot drink or stress, and how you slept if it was a night sweat. Over a couple of weeks the patterns and triggers become clear, especially if you log it next to your HRT.

What triggers hot flashes in menopause?

Common triggers include alcohol, especially red wine, caffeine and very hot drinks, spicy food, stress and strong emotion, warm rooms and heavy bedding, and a poor night of sleep. Triggers are individual, so tracking yours lets you spot which ones actually set yours off.

Does tracking hot flashes help?

Yes. When flushes feel random they feel uncontrollable, and writing them down reveals patterns you would never notice in the moment, such as a reliable trigger or a gradual easing after starting HRT. It also helps you and your prescriber see whether treatment is working.

How long do hot flashes last in menopause?

It varies a lot between people, from a couple of years to several, and for some they linger longer. They often ease over time and HRT reduces them significantly for many people. If flushes are affecting your life, they are very treatable, so it is worth asking for help.
