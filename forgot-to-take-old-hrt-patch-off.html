Forgot to take the old HRT patch off? What happens and what to do

By Balköse Studio · 2026-11-18

If you've found two HRT patches on at once, take the older one off now, fold it in half sticky sides together and bin it. For most people a short overlap won't cause serious harm, though you might feel breast tenderness, nausea or some spotting. If both have been on for more than a day or two, or you feel unwell, ring your pharmacist or NHS 111.

The morning you find two patches

It's Thursday, 6.50am. Jo is 51, runs a team of eight at a housing association, and has a school run in 25 minutes. She's getting dressed when she notices a patch on her left buttock. Fine. Then she spots another one, slightly curled at the edges, on her right.

Her stomach drops. When did she put the new one on? Monday night, she thinks. Or was that last week? The reminder on her phone said "HRT patch" and she did it, half asleep, in the dark. Nothing in that alarm said and take the old one off.

She Googles it with one sock on. The first result is a forum thread where someone says it's fine and someone else says she ended up bleeding for a week. Neither helps. She's now late, anxious, and feels a bit stupid, which is the part that stings most. She used to run project budgets in her head.

A lot of women on menopause forums describe exactly this. The reminder covers the new patch. Nobody reminds you about the old one. And the fog that comes with perimenopause hits the precise kind of memory you need: the "did I already do that?" kind.

What actually happens when you wear two patches

An oestrogen patch (an estradiol patch, if you're reading an American leaflet) releases a steady amount of hormone through your skin over three or four days, or a week for some brands. Two patches means roughly double that release for as long as both are on. The old one will be running low by now, so the overlap is usually smaller than a true double dose, but it's still more than you were prescribed.

What people commonly notice:

  • breasts that feel sore, full or tender
  • mild nausea or a headache
  • bloating or feeling puffy
  • some spotting or a light bleed, especially if you're on sequential HRT

These tend to settle after the extra patch comes off. The NHS lists the same group as common oestrogen side effects in its guide to HRT side effects, which is a useful page to have bookmarked.

What matters more than one slip is the pattern. If it happens once, it's a blip. If it happens every few weeks, your body is getting an uneven dose, and that can show up as symptoms you'd blame on the HRT itself: breakthrough bleeding, swings in mood or flushes coming back. Then you end up at a GP review saying "I don't think it's working", when the real issue was the routine.

How the slip affects the rest of your day

Here's the bit that rarely gets said. The physical side of a double patch is usually small. The mental side isn't.

Finding two patches knocks your confidence. Women on menopause forums describe feeling like they're "losing it", "turning into my mum" or just "useless". It's a bad start to a working day, and if you're already anxious in perimenopause, that loop of what else have I forgotten can run for hours.

There's a practical cost too:

  • Time spent searching forums at 7am instead of getting out the door.
  • A call to the pharmacy you didn't plan for.
  • Patches used faster than your prescription expects, so you run short before the repeat is due, which during a shortage is a real problem.
  • Doubt at your next review, because you can't say for sure how often it's happened.

And there's skin. Two patches means two sites in use, so the rotation gets muddled. Putting a patch back on a spot that hasn't rested is a common reason for red, itchy patches that make people want to give up on patches altogether.

What to do right now: a step-by-step

You can do all of this without any app at all.

  1. Take off the older patch. If you genuinely can't tell, remove the one that looks more lifted or grey at the edges. Fold it sticky sides together so it can't stick to anyone else, and bin it out of reach of children and pets.
  2. Check your brand's leaflet. Look at the change schedule (twice weekly or weekly) so you know when the remaining patch is due.
  3. Write it down. Today's date, the time you noticed, both sites, and roughly how long you think the overlap was.
  4. Note how you feel. Breasts, nausea, headache, bleeding. A one-word note is enough.
  5. Call if you need to. Ring your pharmacist, GP or NHS 111 if you feel unwell, if both were on for more than a day or two, or if you're simply unsure. Pharmacists answer this kind of question all the time and won't think you're daft.
  6. Don't skip or shift doses on your own. Carry on with your usual change days unless a professional tells you otherwise.

If you only remember one thing: take the old one off first, then put the new one on. Make it one movement, never two separate jobs.

A routine that makes patch days impossible to mix up

Here's the routine we'd suggest:

  1. Pick fixed days that match your week, such as Sunday morning and Wednesday night, so changes happen at a natural pause.
  2. Keep patches somewhere you'll see them while undressing, not in a drawer.
  3. Name your alarm with both actions: "OLD patch off, NEW patch on".
  4. Rotate sites in a fixed order: left buttock, right buttock, left lower tummy, right lower tummy.
  5. Log the change straight away, before you've put your top back on.

That last step is the one that saves you on the bad mornings. If the log says Wednesday, right buttock, you know the left one is old.

When it keeps happening

If you've doubled up more than a couple of times in a month, it's worth a conversation with whoever prescribes your HRT. It doesn't mean you're failing. It might mean the delivery method doesn't suit your life.

Some women find a weekly patch easier than a twice-weekly one, simply because there's only one day to remember. Others move to a gel or spray as part of the morning routine, next to the toothbrush, so there's no old one to take off at all. Gel has its own catch, though: you have to let it dry, and it's easy to miss on a rushed day. Neither option is better for everyone.

Bring a record of the last month to that appointment. Dates, sites, any slips, any bleeding. A ten-minute review goes very differently when you can say "I doubled up on the 4th and the 19th, and I spotted on the 21st" instead of "I think I've been a bit all over the place".

Questions people ask

Is it dangerous to wear two HRT patches by accident?

For most people a short overlap is unlikely to cause serious harm, but it is a higher dose than prescribed. Take the old patch off as soon as you notice. If you've had both on for longer than a day or two, feel unwell or have bleeding, ring your pharmacist, GP or NHS 111 for advice.

What side effects might I notice from a double oestrogen dose?

People commonly report sore or swollen breasts, nausea, headaches, bloating or some spotting. These usually settle once the extra patch is off. Write down what you notice and when, so you can tell your pharmacist or GP accurately if you call them.

Should I skip my next patch to make up for it?

Don't change your schedule on your own. Most patient leaflets say to remove the extra patch and carry on with your normal change days. If you're unsure, ask your pharmacist. They can answer this over the counter, usually without an appointment.

How do I tell which patch is the old one?

The older patch often looks slightly lifted at the edges or greyer, but that isn't reliable. The easiest fix is to write the date on each new patch with a fine marker, or log the site and day in a tracker the moment you put it on.

Why do I keep forgetting to take the old patch off?

Perimenopause brain fog affects working memory, which is exactly the kind you need for "did I or didn't I". Reminders tend to cover putting a patch on, not taking one off. Pairing both actions into one step usually fixes it.
