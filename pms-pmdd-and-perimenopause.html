When PMS, PMDD and perimenopause overlap

You have had PMS for years and thought you knew it. Then somewhere in your late thirties or forties it turned into something else: a few days a month where the bottom falls out of your mood, your temper goes, and you genuinely do not feel safe in your own head. For a lot of women that shift is the first real sign that perimenopause has started, long before the hot flushes arrive.

PMS, PMDD and where they sit

Premenstrual syndrome covers the familiar run-up to a period: sore breasts, bloating, low mood, irritability that eases once bleeding starts. Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, or PMDD, is a much more severe version, with intense depression, anxiety or rage in the days before a period that can seriously disrupt your life and relationships. It is a recognised condition, not an overreaction, and it deserves proper care.

Why it gets worse before menopause

Perimenopause is not a smooth decline. Hormones lurch up and down unpredictably, and for many women that turbulence makes premenstrual symptoms louder than they have ever been. You can still be having regular periods and already be deep in this. It catches people out because the usual image of menopause is the end of periods, not years of worse PMS while they continue.

Telling the difference

The timing is the biggest clue. Symptoms that reliably arrive in the second half of your cycle and lift within a day or two of your period starting point towards something hormonal and cyclical. Low mood or anxiety that is there all month, regardless of your cycle, points somewhere else and needs its own assessment. You usually cannot sort this out from memory, which is exactly why writing it down matters.

Why tracking changes everything

If you log your mood and symptoms against your cycle for two or three months, a shape appears that no single bad week could show you. Maybe the crash lands like clockwork five days before you bleed. Maybe the anxiety starts at ovulation. That record does two things at once: it reassures you that there is a pattern rather than chaos, and it gives your GP something concrete to work from instead of a vague “I feel terrible sometimes.”

Charting symptoms across the cycle is part of how PMDD is assessed, so turning up with a couple of months of notes can genuinely speed things along.

Getting help, and when

You do not have to white-knuckle this. There are treatment options, and the right one depends on your symptoms and your history, so it is a conversation for your GP. Go sooner rather than later if these days are affecting your work, your relationships or your sense of safety. And if you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, treat that as urgent and reach out for help straight away.

Start a simple cycle-and-mood log now, before your next period, so you walk into that appointment with the evidence already in your hand.

Common questions

Can PMS get worse in perimenopause?

Yes. Fluctuating hormones in perimenopause often make premenstrual symptoms more intense, and this can happen while your periods are still regular. For many women, worsening PMS is one of the earliest signs of the transition.

What is the difference between PMS and PMDD?

PMS is the common run-up to a period with physical and mood symptoms that ease once it starts. PMDD is a severe, recognised condition with intense depression, anxiety or rage in the days before a period that seriously disrupts daily life. PMDD needs proper medical care.

How do I know if my mood changes are cyclical?

Track your mood against your cycle for two or three months. Symptoms that reliably appear in the second half and lift soon after your period starts are likely cyclical; mood problems present all month need separate assessment. Bring your notes to your GP.
