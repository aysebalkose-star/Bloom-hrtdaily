Living with menopause: getting through the hard days

Some mornings you know before your feet hit the floor. The sheets are damp, your head feels packed with wet sand, and a flat grey mood has moved in without asking. Living with menopause is rarely one long bad stretch. It tends to be decent weeks and then a cluster of days that knock you sideways, usually with no tidy reason.

What a hard day really looks like

It is not always dramatic. Sometimes it is just everything feeling slightly too much at once: the kettle, the group chat, the question your teenager asks while you are mid-thought. A flush climbs up your neck in a meeting and you lose the sentence you were halfway through. By three in the afternoon you want to cry and you could not tell anyone exactly why.

None of that means you are failing at this. It means your hormones are doing something genuinely disruptive, and today you are feeling the brunt of it. Naming the day for what it is, out loud if it helps, takes some of the sting out of it.

Start with the first hour

How the first hour goes often sets the tone, so keep it small and kind. Water before coffee. A few minutes by a window. One thing done, made the bed, fed the cat, so your brain gets a tiny win early. Skip the phone for a bit if you can; the news and everyone else’s highlight reel are a rough way to meet a fragile morning.

If a flush or a broken night has wrecked you, say so to someone before you pretend otherwise. A text to a friend that just says “rough one today” can change the whole shape of the next few hours.

Drop the bar, on purpose

On a hard day the goal is to get through it without making it worse, not to win at life. Pick the two things that genuinely have to happen and let the rest wait. Beans on toast is a dinner. A walk round the block counts as exercise. Answering half your emails is fine.

Lowering the bar deliberately is not giving up. It is how you stop a bad day turning into a bad week because you ran yourself into the ground trying to prove you were coping.

Mind the good days too

Here is the part most of us get wrong. When a good day arrives we cram it full, catching up on everything we missed, and then wonder why we crash. Treat the good days as something to protect, not strip-mine. Do the thing you enjoy. Bank some rest. Get to bed at a reasonable hour even though you feel fine, because sleep is the first thing menopause comes for.

Working out what tips you over

After a while, patterns start to show if you let them. Maybe the worst days land in the week before a bleed. Maybe it is the second glass of wine, or the nights you scroll until midnight, or skipping lunch because you were busy. You will almost never spot these in the moment. Written down over a few weeks, though, the usual suspects tend to give themselves away, and then you have something you can actually work with.

Jot the plain facts each day: how you slept, what you ate, whether you moved, where you were in your cycle, how the day felt out of ten. It takes seconds and it turns “why do I feel awful again” into a question you can answer.

Common questions

Why are some menopause days so much worse than others?

Hormone levels swing around in perimenopause rather than dropping smoothly, so you can feel fine one day and flattened the next. Poor sleep, where you are in your cycle, stress, alcohol and skipping meals can all tip a borderline day over. Tracking often reveals the pattern.

How do I cope on a really bad menopause day?

Keep the first hour small and kind, drop your to-do list to the two things that must happen, and tell someone you are having a rough one. The aim is to get through without making it worse, not to perform at your usual level.

Should I push through menopause symptoms or rest?

Resting on a bad day often prevents it snowballing into a bad week. If symptoms are frequent, severe or stopping you living your life, that is worth a conversation with your GP rather than something to simply endure.
