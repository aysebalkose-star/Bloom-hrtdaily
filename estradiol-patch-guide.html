Estradiol patches, explained simply

The first time you hold one, it's oddly underwhelming — a small, clear square in a foil packet, and you're meant to trust it with how you feel for the next few days. If you've just been prescribed an estradiol patch, or you're weighing it up, here's a warm, plain-English walk through how they work and the little questions nobody quite answers at the appointment.

What an estradiol patch actually does

An estradiol patch delivers estrogen — the hormone that drops away in menopause — straight through your skin and into your bloodstream, slowly and steadily. That steadiness is the whole point: instead of the peaks and dips you can get from a daily tablet, the patch keeps a gentle, even level going, which many people find smooths out symptoms like hot flushes, night sweats, mood dips and that worn-out, not-quite-myself feeling.

Why some people prefer patches over tablets

It comes down to how the hormone gets in. Because a patch goes through the skin rather than through the stomach and liver, the evidence suggests transdermal estrogen (patches, gels and sprays) may carry a lower risk of blood clots than estrogen taken as tablets — which is why patches are often suggested for some people in particular. That's a conversation to have with your own prescriber about your own history, but it's one of the main reasons patches are so widely used. Some people simply prefer not having another pill to remember, too.

How often you change it

This is the one that catches everyone out, because it varies by brand. Many estradiol patches are changed twice a week — roughly every three to four days — while some are once-weekly. Neither is better; they're just different products. The trick is to pin yours to fixed days (for a twice-weekly patch, something like Monday and Thursday) so it becomes a rhythm instead of a rolling count you have to keep in your head.

Where and how to apply it

  • Clean, dry, cool skin. Below the waist is usual — the lower tummy or the hip/bottom area. Skip anywhere you've just put on cream or oil.
  • Press it on firmly for a good ten seconds, smoothing the edges.
  • Not on the breasts, and usually not on the waistline where clothing rubs.
  • Rotate the spot each time to keep skin happy — and, handily, alternating sides can help you remember which change you're on.

Always check your own patch's leaflet, as the details differ slightly between brands.

If it keeps peeling off

Patches that lift at the edges are a common annoyance, especially in heat, with sweat, or after swimming. Applying to properly dry skin, pressing firmly, and avoiding moisturiser underneath all help. If one falls off, your leaflet will say whether to reapply it or use a fresh one — and if it keeps happening, mention it, because a different brand or a gel might simply suit your skin better.

Patches and progesterone

If you still have your womb, estrogen is almost always paired with progesterone to protect the lining — that might be a separate capsule, a combined patch, or a hormonal coil. It's an important piece of the picture, and worth being clear on with your prescriber. (We go into it more in how to track your menopause HRT.)

Keeping track without the mental load

Whatever your schedule, the easiest way to stay on top of a patch is to let something else hold the dates. Logging each change and planning the month ahead means you always know where you are — and, just as usefully, you build a quiet record of how you're feeling as your body settles in, which is gold at your next review.

Common questions

How long does an estradiol patch last?

It depends on the brand. Many estradiol patches are changed twice a week, roughly every three to four days, and some are changed once a week. Your patch leaflet or prescriber will tell you which type yours is, and setting fixed change days makes it much easier to keep track.

Where do you put an estradiol patch?

Usually on clean, dry, cool skin below the waist, such as the lower tummy or the hip and bottom area, pressed on firmly for about ten seconds. Avoid the breasts and the waistline where clothes rub, and rotate the spot each time. Always follow your own patch leaflet, as brands vary.

Are estradiol patches safer than tablets?

Because a patch delivers estrogen through the skin rather than through the stomach and liver, evidence suggests transdermal estrogen may carry a lower risk of blood clots than estrogen tablets. Whether a patch is right for you depends on your own history, so discuss it with your prescriber.

What should I do if my estradiol patch falls off?

Check your patch leaflet, which will say whether to reapply the same patch or use a fresh one. Applying to dry skin, pressing firmly and avoiding moisturiser underneath all help patches stick. If it keeps happening, tell your prescriber, as a different brand or a gel may suit your skin better.

Do I need progesterone with an estradiol patch?

If you still have your womb, estrogen is almost always combined with progesterone to protect the womb lining, whether as a separate capsule, a combined patch or a hormonal coil. If you have had a hysterectomy you may not need it. Your prescriber will advise what is right for you.
