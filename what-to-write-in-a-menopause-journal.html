What to actually write in a menopause journal

Everyone tells you to keep a journal and almost no one tells you what to put in it. So you buy the nice notebook, write a heartfelt page on day one, miss day two, feel guilty, and quietly give up by the weekend. A menopause journal that actually helps looks nothing like that. It is shorter, duller, and far more useful.

A quick note: this is general, educational writing, not medical advice. Anything to do with HRT, medication or a diagnosis belongs with your own GP or specialist, so please take your questions to them.

Why bother at all

Memory is the first casualty of a bad menopause week, which is a problem, because memory is exactly what you rely on when someone asks how you have been. A journal is not about deep reflection or beautiful prose. It is a way of outsourcing the remembering, so that the pattern of your own body is written down somewhere you can actually see it.

Keep it short enough to survive

The journals that last are the ones you can finish in under a minute. If it feels like homework, you will stop. Aim for a handful of quick notes a day rather than a page, and let yourself be scrappy. Done badly every day beats done beautifully for a week and then abandoned.

What to jot each day

You do not need all of this, but it is a good menu to pick from:

  • how you slept, roughly, and whether night sweats woke you
  • mood and energy, even just a number out of ten
  • any symptoms that stood out: flushes, aches, palpitations, anxiety, fog
  • where you are in your cycle, if you are still bleeding
  • what you took: HRT, supplements, anything new
  • the ordinary stuff that moves the needle: food, alcohol, movement, stress

That last line matters more than people expect. The second glass of wine, the skipped lunch, the brutal week at work; these leave fingerprints all over how you feel, and you will only catch them written down.

Reading it back is the point

Writing is only half of it. Every week or two, read back over what you wrote. That is where the value lives: the realisation that the worst days cluster at a certain point in your cycle, that your sleep nosedived the week you started something new, that you are, quietly, better than you were a month ago. On a grim day, that evidence is a kind of lifeline. It tells you this passes, because last time it did.

Turning it into a better appointment

When you see your GP, your journal does the heavy lifting. Instead of “I think things are a bit better, maybe,” you can say your flushes have dropped from most of the day to a couple, but your sleep is still broken. That is the kind of detail that gets you a real plan instead of a holding pattern, whether you are weighing up HRT, adjusting a dose, or working out what else might be going on.

You can keep all of this in a notebook. The reason a lot of women move it onto their phone is simple: it is always with you, a note takes one tap, and nothing is sitting on the kitchen table for everyone to read. However you do it, start tonight with three lines, and let it build from there.

Common questions

What should I write in a menopause journal?

Keep it short: how you slept, your mood and energy out of ten, any standout symptoms, where you are in your cycle, what you took, and lifestyle factors like food, alcohol, movement and stress. A handful of quick notes a day is plenty.

How do I keep a menopause journal without giving up?

Make it fast enough to finish in under a minute and let it be scrappy. Done badly every day is far more useful than a beautiful entry once a week. The habit matters more than the writing.

How does a journal help at my menopause appointment?

It replaces ‘I think it is a bit better’ with specifics, like flushes dropping from all day to a couple but sleep still broken. That detail helps your GP give you a real plan rather than a holding pattern.
