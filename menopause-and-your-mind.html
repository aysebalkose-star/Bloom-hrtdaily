It’s not just hot flashes: menopause and your mind

The flushes you can explain. But the anxiety that arrives from nowhere, the flatness, the days your own brain feels like a stranger’s — those are harder to say out loud. Menopause touches the mind as much as the body, and it is not a weakness. Let’s talk about it honestly.

The part no one prepares you for

Many women say the mental and emotional changes were the hardest and most frightening part of menopause — and the least talked about. When your mood, memory and sense of self wobble, it’s easy to assume you’re failing at life, rather than living through a hormonal shift. You are not failing.

Why hormones hit your mind

Oestrogen interacts with brain chemistry involved in mood, memory and stress. As levels fall and fluctuate, it’s understandable that anxiety, low mood and fog can follow. This is biology, not character — and knowing that can lift some of the shame that makes it worse.

What it can look like

It shows up in many shapes: a new, humming anxiety; tearfulness or rage that doesn’t fit the trigger; a flat greyness where joy used to be; losing your words mid-sentence; dreading things you used to do easily. None of these mean you’re broken.

When to take it seriously

Low mood that lifts is one thing; low mood that doesn’t is another. If you feel persistently hopeless, can’t function, or have any thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out to your GP or urgent help now — this matters more than any tracker. Perimenopausal mood changes are treatable, and you deserve support quickly.

Holding on to yourself

Part of coping is evidence: when you can see that the dark days cluster at certain times, or ease with certain changes, the fog feels less like a verdict and more like something you can work with. Note your mood alongside your sleep, cycle, treatments, supplements and daily life — and take that record to your GP. You’re not complaining; you’re giving them what they need to help you.

Be gentle with yourself while you do it. The woman you are hasn’t gone anywhere — she’s moving through a storm, and storms pass.

Common questions

Can menopause cause anxiety and depression?

Yes — falling and fluctuating oestrogen can affect brain chemistry involved in mood and stress, and anxiety, low mood and brain fog are recognised features of perimenopause. They are treatable, so it’s worth speaking to your GP.

Is menopause brain fog real?

Yes. Trouble with memory, focus and word-finding is a genuine and common feature of perimenopause, linked to hormonal change — not a sign of failing or early dementia.

When should I get help for menopause-related low mood?

If low mood is persistent, stops you functioning, or you have any thoughts of harming yourself, contact your GP or urgent help straight away. Perimenopausal mood changes are treatable and you deserve support quickly.
