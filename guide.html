How to track your menopause HRT: a simple guide

Whether you call it HRT, hormone replacement therapy, or menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), keeping a simple record of your doses and how you feel is one of the most useful things you can do. This guide explains the types of hormone therapy, the common delivery methods, and exactly what's worth tracking — in plain English.

A quick note: this is general educational information, not medical advice, and HRT Daily is a personal tracking tool, not a medical device. Everyone's menopause and HRT is different — always follow the instructions from your own doctor, GP, or prescriber, and speak to them before changing anything.

What HRT actually is

Around perimenopause and menopause, the ovaries gradually make less estrogen (spelled oestrogen in the UK) and progesterone. That drop is what drives the familiar symptoms — hot flashes (hot flushes), night sweats, disrupted sleep, mood changes, brain fog, joint aches and vaginal dryness, among others.

HRT simply tops up those hormones to ease symptoms. Most regimens are built around two hormones: estrogen to relieve symptoms, and a progestogen (often micronised progesterone) to protect the lining of the womb if you still have a uterus. Some people are also prescribed a small amount of testosterone, usually for low libido.

HRT, hormone therapy, MHT — what's the difference?

They're the same thing, just named differently by region:

  • HRT (hormone replacement therapy) — the everyday term in the UK, Ireland, Australia and much of the world.
  • Hormone therapy / HT and MHT (menopausal hormone therapy) — increasingly preferred by clinicians in the US.

If you're reading US and UK sources side by side, you'll also see spelling differences — estrogen/oestrogen, hot flashes/hot flushes, fetal/foetal. They mean exactly the same. HRT Daily uses both, so nothing gets lost in translation.

The types of HRT

Estrogen (oestrogen)

The main symptom-relieving hormone. Body-identical estradiol is the most common form today and comes as patches, gels, sprays, tablets and implants.

Progesterone / progestogen

Taken alongside estrogen to protect the womb lining if you have a uterus. Micronised progesterone (often taken at night) is widely used; it can also be delivered by an intrauterine system. People who've had a hysterectomy often take estrogen on its own.

Testosterone

A lower-profile part of HRT, sometimes added — most often as a cream or gel — to help with persistent low sex drive when estrogen alone hasn't resolved it.

Because a regimen can combine two or three hormones on different schedules, it's easy to lose track of what you took and when. Giving each hormone its own colour — as HRT Daily does — makes the whole routine legible at a glance.

Delivery methods, and why they matter for tracking

The method changes how often you dose, which is the thing a tracker is really keeping tabs on:

  • Patches — usually changed once or twice a week (e.g. every 3–4 days). Easy to forget which day you're on.
  • Gels & sprays — applied daily to the skin.
  • Tablets — taken daily; progesterone is often taken nightly.
  • Implants / pellets — inserted by a clinician and last for months (often around every 12 weeks), so the "dose" you're tracking is really the renewal date.

A good tracker handles all of these — from twice-daily to once-every-twelve-weeks — and shows you what's due today without any mental arithmetic.

Cyclical vs continuous regimens

Broadly, HRT is prescribed in two patterns, and which one you're on affects what you'll want to log:

  • Cyclical (sequential) HRT — often used in perimenopause when you're still having periods. Progesterone is taken for part of each month, and a monthly bleed is expected. Here, planning exact dates matters.
  • Continuous combined HRT — usually used after menopause. Both hormones are taken every day with no scheduled bleed.

A tap-to-select calendar makes cyclical routines far easier, because you can lay out the exact days for each hormone a month at a time.

Why tracking your HRT is worth it

Three practical reasons:

  1. You actually take it as intended. Twice-weekly patches and nightly progesterone are surprisingly easy to miss. Seeing "due today" removes the guesswork.
  2. You can see whether it's helping. Symptoms improve gradually, so week-to-week it's hard to feel progress. A simple daily rating turns a vague impression into a visible trend.
  3. Your review appointments get better. Instead of trying to remember the last three months, you can show your prescriber a clear record of doses and symptoms — which makes dose adjustments faster and more accurate.

What's actually worth tracking

You don't need to log everything. A useful minimum:

  • Doses — what you took and when, for each hormone.
  • Core symptoms — hot flashes (hot flushes), night sweats, sleep quality, mood, anxiety, energy, brain fog, joint aches, and vaginal dryness.
  • Bleeding — especially on cyclical HRT, or any unexpected bleeding (which is always worth mentioning to your prescriber).
  • Side effects — anything new after a dose or brand change, so you can spot patterns.
  • Milestones — the good ones too: the first week you slept through, when the flashes eased.

Rating a handful of symptoms takes seconds a day, and a rolling 7-day view is usually enough to see the direction of travel.

How do I know if my HRT is working?

Timelines vary from person to person, but as a rough guide: hot flashes and night sweats often start easing within a few weeks, while sleep, mood and energy can take up to around three months to settle as the dose is fine-tuned. Vaginal and urinary symptoms treated with local (vaginal) estrogen can take a little longer.

The honest answer is that "working" is best judged over weeks, not days — which is exactly why a written trend beats memory. If symptoms aren't improving, or side effects are bothering you, that's a conversation to have with your prescriber rather than something to adjust yourself.

Common questions

What's the difference between HRT and hormone therapy?

They're the same treatment. "HRT" is the common term in the UK and much of the world; in the US, clinicians increasingly say "hormone therapy", "menopausal hormone therapy" or "MHT". All mean replacing the estrogen (oestrogen), and usually progesterone, the body makes less of around menopause.

Why do I need progesterone with my estrogen?

If you still have your uterus, a progestogen such as micronised progesterone is usually taken alongside estrogen to protect the lining of the womb. People who've had a hysterectomy often take estrogen alone. Your prescriber decides which regimen is right for you.

How long does HRT take to work?

Many people notice hot flashes and night sweats easing within a few weeks, while sleep, mood and other symptoms can take up to about three months to settle. Tracking symptoms over time is the clearest way to see the trend and to have an informed conversation with your prescriber.

What should I do if I miss a dose?

Guidance depends on the type of HRT — a patch, gel, tablet or pellet each behave differently — so follow the leaflet that came with your medicine, or ask your pharmacist or prescriber. A tracker helps by showing exactly when your last dose was, so you're not guessing.
